The Textbook Construction

How do you compare portfolios that differ in both return and risk?

What problem is this solving?

Investors face a tradeoff between expected return and risk. Higher returns generally come with higher volatility. So how do you compare two portfolios when one has higher expected return but also higher volatility? You can’t say one is “better” without some way of trading off return against risk.

The Sharpe ratio is one common answer. It measures excess return per unit of risk — specifically, the portfolio’s return above the risk-free rate, divided by its volatility. A higher Sharpe ratio means more return for each unit of risk taken. By this measure, the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio is the most efficient — sometimes called the tangency portfolio.

The widget below shows how the tangency portfolio is constructed geometrically. The frontier curve traces every possible stocks/bonds mix; the capital allocation line picks out the one with the highest Sharpe ratio.

A note on what the Sharpe ratio is and isn’t: it’s the standard return-per-unit-of-risk measure, but it has known limitations — it treats upside and downside volatility symmetrically, and it assumes returns are roughly normally distributed (they aren’t, exactly). Despite these limitations, it remains widely used in practice as an intuitive summary, and it’s a useful starting point for thinking about the return-risk tradeoff. The construction

The frontier curve shows every possible (volatility, return) pair you can achieve by mixing stocks and bonds. Each point on the curve is one allocation — from 100% bonds on the left to 100% stocks on the right.

The capital allocation line (CAL) is the straight line from the risk-free rate to the point where it just touches the frontier. That touch point is the tangency portfolio — the allocation that maximizes the Sharpe ratio.

Why “tangency”? The Sharpe ratio is the slope of a line from the risk-free rate to any point on the frontier. The CAL is the steepest such line that still reaches the frontier — it’s tangent to the curve. The tangency portfolio is where that line touches the frontier, and its slope is the highest achievable Sharpe ratio.

Things to try

Move the sliders to see how the construction responds. Try increasing stock volatility — the frontier stretches rightward and the tangency point shifts. Lower the correlation — the frontier becomes more concave (diversification becomes more powerful). Raise the risk-free rate — the tangency point slides along the frontier toward bonds.
